MSc dissertationSep 2026

Landslide inventory, susceptibility and exposure along the NH-5 Satluj corridor

NH-5 follows the Satluj Valley through some of the steepest terrain in the western Himalaya. I mapped where slopes have failed along the road, modelled where failure is relatively more likely across the district, and measured which land and villages sit in the way.

Read the dissertation (PDF, 5 MB)
179landslides hand-digitised in the 1 km corridor
57.0%of them within 300 m of the road
91village points inside the corridor
28.7%of Kinnaur classed High or Very High susceptibility

The question

How are landslides distributed along the highway, what pattern of relative susceptibility do terrain and environmental factors produce, and what land and settlements are exposed?

Data

  • High-resolution Google Earth imagery, November 2025
  • ALOS DEM, 12.5 m (slope, elevation, aspect, stream order)
  • Esri / Impact Observatory Sentinel-2 land cover, 10 m
  • Geological Survey of India geology, 1:50,000
  • NBSS&LUP soil texture, 1:50,000
  • CHRS PERSIANN-CDR precipitation, 2025, 0.25°
  • Survey of India road and village points, 1:50,000

What I did

  1. Hand-digitised 179 landslide polygons from November 2025 imagery inside a 1,000 m corridor around NH-5, and grouped them by area into 58 small, 104 medium and 17 large.
  2. Split the corridor into three non-overlapping bands (0–300, 300–600 and 600–1,000 m) in WGS 1984 UTM zone 44N and counted landslides and landslide area in each band.
  3. Rasterised the landslides at 10 m and overlaid them on Sentinel-2 land cover to measure what they cover, then mapped the village points that fall inside the corridor.
  4. Reclassified eight conditioning factors and combined them in a weighted overlay across 6,482 km² of Kinnaur, weighting slope highest.
  5. Ran a separate Kriging surface of landslide area and kept it apart from the susceptibility model as an exploratory layer.

Factor weights in the susceptibility model

Slope35%
Elevation15%
Precipitation15%
Geology10%
Land cover10%
Aspect5%
Soil texture5%
Stream order5%

What I found

  • Landslides thin out with distance from the road: 102 (57.0%) lie within 300 m, 44 (24.6%) at 300–600 m and 33 (18.4%) at 600–1,000 m.
  • In the corridor, landslides overlap 6.30 km² of land cover, mostly rangeland (58.1%) and bare ground (38.1%). Built-up land makes up 3.1%.
  • 91 village points sit inside the corridor, including Pangi, Powari, Tapri and Urni.
  • Across Kinnaur, Medium susceptibility covers 44.6% of the district, High 26.1% and Very High 2.6%.

Limits I flagged

  • The susceptibility map is relative and expert-weighted, with no statistical validation such as ROC/AUC, so it shows where conditions combine rather than a probability of failure.
  • The road-proximity pattern is a spatial association. It does not prove that road construction caused the failures.
  • My April 2025 field photographs give context only and were not used as validation.

Landsat time seriesJan 2026

Two decades of vegetation disturbance and recovery on steep Himalayan slopes

Vegetation holds thin mountain soils in place, and losing it on steep ground raises landslide risk. Using annual Landsat composites from 2000 to 2024, I tracked when and how badly vegetation was lost on Himachal Pradesh’s steep slopes, how long it took to recover, and where those signals overlap.

Read the report (PDF, 3.4 MB)
2000–2024annual Landsat NDVI composites
30°slope threshold for landslide-relevant terrain
12districts ranked by hotspot area
1.71 km²hotspot area in Chamba, the highest

The question

How much vegetation disturbance has there been since 2000, does it concentrate on steep slopes and particular elevation bands, and which districts show the strongest combined stress?

Data

  • Landsat annual NDVI composites, 2000–2024 (Google Earth Engine)
  • SRTM DEM (slope and elevation bands)
  • CHIRPS monsoon rainfall, 2000–2024
  • District boundaries for Himachal Pradesh

What I did

  1. Built annual Landsat NDVI composites for 2000–2024 in Google Earth Engine to damp cloud, atmospheric and seasonal noise.
  2. Ran the LandTrendr segmentation algorithm to extract the year of greatest disturbance, its magnitude (NDVI loss) and the recovery time.
  3. Kept disturbance on slopes of 30° or more from the SRTM DEM and stratified it by elevation band.
  4. Mapped the long-term trend with Sen’s slope and compared disturbed steep slopes against nearby stable terrain.
  5. Intersected steep terrain, disturbance or slow recovery, and a negative long-term trend into a deliberately conservative hotspot layer, summed it by district, and repeated it at a 25° threshold to test sensitivity.
  6. Added CHIRPS monsoon rainfall anomalies as context only, never as a classifier.

What I found

  • Disturbance is episodic and clustered rather than a steady statewide decline, with the strongest losses and the slowest recovery on steep mid- to high-elevation slopes.
  • Chamba has the largest hotspot area (1.71 km²), followed by Kullu (0.68 km²) and Mandi (0.54 km²). Una has none.
  • The spatial pattern and the district ranking held when the slope threshold was lowered to 25°.

Limits I flagged

  • No landslide inventory was available to validate against, so hotspots mark compounded environmental stress, not landslide occurrence or probability.
  • Subsurface factors such as lithology and soil were left out, and Landsat’s 30 m pixels miss narrow road cuts and small scars.

Research placementFeb–Apr 2026

Neighbourhood context for a park evaluation in West Gorton, Manchester

A co-designed park improvement in West Gorton was followed by large rises in walking and park use. The research team needed to know whether that could just reflect new people moving into the area. I built the neighbourhood evidence to test it, against two comparison areas.

12LSOAs harmonised from 2011 to 2021 boundaries
8.2%of West Gorton residents moved in the year before the census
14.4%population growth, 2018–2024
Decile 1every West Gorton LSOA in the most deprived 10%

The question

Did West Gorton and its comparison areas differ in population change, residential churn, house prices, deprivation and social profile, and could those differences explain the rise in park use?

Data

  • ONS small-area population estimates, 2018–2024
  • ONS House Price Statistics for Small Areas, dataset 46, 2018–2023
  • English Indices of Deprivation 2025 (with a cautious comparison to 2019)
  • Census 2021: TS007A age, TS021 ethnicity, TS054 tenure, TS019 migration, TS038 disability
  • ONS LSOA 2011 to 2021 exact-fit lookup

What I did

  1. Harmonised the study areas to 12 Census 2021 LSOAs with the ONS exact-fit lookup, resolving the splits and merges between the 2011 and 2021 releases.
  2. Cleaned every source into join-ready tables and joined them on the 2021 LSOA code in ArcGIS Pro.
  3. Carried older house-price medians across to split or merged areas and treated them as indicative, because medians cannot simply be re-added.
  4. Produced thematic maps and summary tables for population, house prices, deprivation, tenure, mobility, age, ethnicity and disability.
  5. Wrote the findings up for non-technical readers.

What I found

  • West Gorton had the lowest churn: 8.2% of residents had moved in the year before the census, against 24.2% in Ardwick and 30.3% in Pendleton.
  • Its population grew 14.4% between 2018 and 2024, against 20.8% in Ardwick and 56.0% in Pendleton.
  • All three West Gorton LSOAs fall in the most deprived 10% nationally on IMD 2025, and 53.1% of households rent socially.
  • House prices rose least there (a mean LSOA change of 16.6%, against 30.4% and 41.8%), so there is no strong sign of rapid green gentrification over the period.
  • Together these point to rising park use coming from the existing community rather than from new residents. The analysis now informs the team’s research with community members and Manchester City Council.

Limits I flagged

  • LSOAs are larger than the park’s catchment, so this describes the neighbourhood, not the people using the park.
  • Census variables are a 2021 snapshot, and IMD releases are relative rankings rather than a clean time series.

UAV surveyJan 2026

UAV canopy survey of a beaver reintroduction site, Cornwall

Beavers fell trees and flood ground, which should open up a woodland canopy. I compared canopy gaps in a beaver-influenced woodland and a woodland without beavers, using drone imagery and a height model, to see whether their structure differs.

Read the report (PDF, 2.3 MB)
2woodlands surveyed by UAV
7 mheight threshold for a canopy gap
13.96 vs 15.08median gap shape complexity, beaver vs none
203 vs 218predicted understory species (±12), beaver vs none

The question

Does canopy-gap structure differ between beaver and non-beaver woodland, and what does a gap-based proxy suggest for understory plant richness?

Data

  • UAV imagery captured during fieldwork, with ground control points
  • Structure-from-Motion orthomosaics and digital surface models
  • British National Grid (EPSG:27700) throughout

What I did

  1. Flew the survey over both woodlands under staff supervision and set ground control points to keep the two sites spatially consistent.
  2. Digitised each woodland boundary from the orthomosaics to limit edge effects.
  3. Interpolated a terrain model from ground points with IDW and subtracted it from the SfM surface model to get height above ground.
  4. Classified anything under 7 m as canopy gap, checked the result against the orthomosaics and converted the gaps to polygons.
  5. Measured area and perimeter for every gap, calculated the Gap Shape Complexity Index for the three largest gaps per site, and applied the provided model to predict understory richness.

What I found

  • The beaver site has one dominant, continuous opening. The woodland without beavers has many smaller, fragmented gaps.
  • Median Gap Shape Complexity was 13.96 at the beaver site and 15.08 without beavers, so the dominant gaps there are slightly simpler in shape.
  • The proxy model predicts about 203 (±12) understory species at the beaver site and 218 (±12) without, so larger gaps did not translate into higher predicted richness.

Limits I flagged

  • Richness is a modelled proxy, not a field count, and the model was built in a different woodland type.
  • A fixed 7 m threshold and only three gaps per site make the result sensitive to shadows and to how boundaries were drawn.
  • With two sites, beaver activity is a likely contributing factor, not a proven cause.

Suitability modellingJan 2026

Priority locations for green space interventions in Greater Manchester

Green space does the most for health where children and deprivation are concentrated, but access and greenness are uneven across the city. I combined four layers into one suitability model to show where green space interventions would do the most good.

Read the report (PDF, 3 MB)
4criteria combined, each normalised to 0–1
5suitability classes, from Very Low to Very High
0.395mean LSOA suitability (range 0.138 to 0.637)
10 mSentinel-2 NDVI resolution

The question

Where within Greater Manchester should green space interventions be targeted to give the greatest benefit, especially for children and deprived neighbourhoods?

Data

  • OS Open Greenspace (2024)
  • Sentinel-2 bands 4 and 8 for NDVI, 10 m
  • Index of Multiple Deprivation 2019
  • Census 2021 child population (TS007A)
  • LSOA 2011 boundaries and the OpenPopGrid population grid

What I did

  1. Turned distance to the nearest public green space into an access-deficit layer.
  2. Calculated NDVI from Sentinel-2 bands 4 and 8 and inverted it, so low vegetation scores as high need.
  3. Normalised deprivation and child population to 0–1 alongside the two environmental layers.
  4. Combined the four layers with a weighted linear model, giving deprivation and children the higher weights to put equity first.
  5. Classified the surface into five classes, extracted the Very High zones and aggregated scores to LSOAs with zonal statistics.

What I found

  • High suitability clusters in inner-urban Manchester and neighbouring boroughs, where poor access and low vegetation coincide with deprivation and more children.
  • The priority areas are not simply the places with the least green space. They are where several disadvantages overlap.
  • Mean suitability by LSOA ranged from 0.138 to 0.637, with a mean of 0.395 and a median of 0.406.

Limits I flagged

  • Straight-line distance overstates access where railways, rivers or main roads get in the way. Network walking distance would be the next step.
  • NDVI is from a single date, the data come from different years, and the weights were not sensitivity-tested.

SAR flood mappingJun 2026

Mapping the October 2024 Valencia flood with Sentinel-1 radar

Cloud and darkness block optical satellites during a flood, but radar sees through both. I tested how much of the October 2024 Valencia flood a simple Sentinel-1 change-detection workflow could recover, against the official Copernicus emergency map.

35.62 km²of strong backscatter decrease detected
34.90 km²of it inside the official flooded area
22.8%of the official flooded area captured
156.12 km²flooded area in the Copernicus product

The question

How well does a simple Sentinel-1 backscatter change-detection approach identify flood-related change, compared with the Copernicus EMS delineation?

Data

  • Sentinel-1 GRD, VV and VH, 19 and 31 October 2024
  • Copernicus EMS EMSR773, AOI01 Valencia Province (flooded area and flood trace)

What I did

  1. Matched Sentinel-1 GRD scenes from before and after the event in Google Earth Engine, keeping orbit conditions and VV/VH polarisations consistent.
  2. Smoothed speckle, converted from decibels to power and built a combined pre/post backscatter-decrease ratio.
  3. Thresholded the ratio into a change mask and compared it with the Copernicus flooded-area and flood-trace polygons.

What I found

  • Copernicus mapped 156.12 km² of flooded area and 374.28 km² of flood trace.
  • My mask found 35.62 km² of strong backscatter decrease, and 34.90 km² of it fell inside the official flooded area, so what it detected was almost all real.
  • It captured only about 22.8% of the official flooded area and under 10% of the flood trace. Urban flooding was the hard part.

Limits I flagged

  • Buildings, vegetation and mixed surfaces hide or distort the flood signal, so the mask is a high-confidence open-water layer, not a complete flood map.

Spatial algorithms in Python: geodesic borders and weighted redistribution

Two problems solved in code rather than by clicking through a GIS: a scalable search for the world’s shortest international border, and an implementation of weighted redistribution that turns badly geocoded points into an honest surface.

Shortest international border report (PDF) Weighted redistribution of geocoded tweets report (PDF)

w = 50, s = 0.05redistribution parameters chosen after a sweep

November 2025

Shortest international border

I wrote an algorithm to find the shortest land border in the Natural Earth country polygons (ne_10m_admin_0_countries).

  1. Loaded the countries into a GeoDataFrame and built an STRtree spatial index, so only pairs whose bounding boxes overlap are tested instead of every possible pair.
  2. Used geometry intersection to separate real shared borders (lines) from single-point touches, which are excluded.
  3. Measured each border as a geodesic length on the WGS84 ellipsoid with pyproj’s Geod, because projected distance would understate it.
  4. Mapped the result in a Lambert conformal conic projection with GeoPandas and Matplotlib.
  • Italy–Vatican City, at 0.43 km, is the shortest international border in the dataset.
January 2026

Weighted redistribution of geocoded tweets

Tweets geocoded only to their district stack up on the same points and create false hotspots. I implemented Huck et al.’s weighted redistribution to relocate each tweet within its own district, guided by population density, and turn the points into a continuous surface.

  1. Projected everything to British National Grid so areas and radii work in metres.
  2. Generated candidate locations inside each district by bounding-box rejection sampling, so no tweet can leave its own district.
  3. Scored w candidates against a population raster and kept the densest, then spread each tweet as a fuzzy splat whose radius comes from district area and the fuzziness parameter s.
  4. Swept s ∈ {0.03, 0.05} and w ∈ {25, 50, 100} and chose w = 50, s = 0.05 as the balance between random scatter and over-peaked surfaces.
def relocate_weighted(point, polygon, raster, w):
    best, best_val = None, -1
    for _ in range(w):
        cand = random_point_in_polygon(polygon)
        val = raster_value(cand, raster)
        if val > best_val:
            best_val, best = val, cand
    return best
  • The false district-centroid hotspots disappear, and the surface follows population while respecting district boundaries.

Limits I flagged

  • Natural Earth borders are generalised, so 0.43 km reflects the dataset rather than a surveyed border.
  • Redistribution treats population as the only proxy for tweeting, results vary between runs unless a seed is fixed, and parameters were chosen by visual checks.
